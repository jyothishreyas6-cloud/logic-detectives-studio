FOR GROWN-UPS
Learning goal: add and subtract 2-digit numbers with regrouping, using the rule "Ten ones and one ten are worth the same." (38 + 27: 8 ones and 7 ones make 15 ones, so ten of them regroup into one ten, giving 6 tens and 5 ones, 65. Checking with 65 take away 27 means regrouping one ten back into ten ones.)
Curriculum link: NSW Mathematics K-10 Syllabus (2022), Stage 2 (Years 3 and 4), Additive relations, MA2-AR-01: "selects and uses mental and written strategies for addition and subtraction involving 2- and 3-digit numbers". https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/mathematics/mathematics-k-10-2022/outcomes
Ask your child after watching: "Where did Otto's missing ten go?"
Try it together: bundle ten pegs (or pencils) with a rubber band to make one ten, then add two groups of pegs and bundle any ten loose ones.

"Oh no! The till won't balance!" The sheet and the money don't match.

WHAT YOUR CHILD WILL LEARN
- Regroup means swapping ten ones for one ten, like ten one-dollar coins for a ten-dollar note.
- When the ones add up to ten or more, ten of them become one more ten: 38 + 27 = 65, not 55.
- Subtracting can need regrouping too: in 65 take away 27, one ten becomes ten ones.
- Checking an addition by taking away is a second way to prove the answer.

WHAT HAPPENS IN THIS CASE
At the surf club kiosk at the beach, Otto has added up the till and the sheet and the money don't match. The narrator explains that regroup means swapping ten ones for one ten, like ten one-dollar coins for a ten-dollar note. The till started with 38 dollars and the kiosk sold 27 dollars of icy poles. Otto rushes: eight and seven make fifteen, so he writes the five, and gets 55 dollars. Ivy wonders where the ten went. Detective Bramble says, "Let's not guess. Let's test." Your child is asked to point to the one-dollar coins, then to choose how much should be in the till, with five quiet seconds to think. On the counting mat, eight coins and seven coins make fifteen ones; ten of them are swapped for one ten-dollar note, leaving six tens and five ones: 65 dollars. Taking the sales away proves it a second way: 65 take away 27 leaves 38, just what the till started with. Case solved! Then Otto offers to "carry the ten" and balances the note on his head like a surfboard. At the end, your child is invited to say the rule out loud: "Ten ones and one ten are worth the same."

KEY WORDS

TRY IT AT HOME
Bundle ten pegs with a rubber band to make one ten. Make 38 (three bundles and eight loose pegs) and 27 (two bundles and seven loose pegs). Push them together, bundle any ten loose pegs, and count the bundles and the loose pegs. Then take 27 away again and see that you get back to 38.
